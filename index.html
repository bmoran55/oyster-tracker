Citizen science on tidal water

Growing a living reef, cage by cage, on the Potomac.

Every spring we take in a new cohort of about 500 baby oysters and raise them off our dock till they are ready to add to our reef or just eat. This site is the field journal — the numbers, the photos, and the occasional blue crab that gets in where it shouldn't.

Why we garden

One float, five hundred oysters, and a river that needs them.

Oyster gardening is simple in principle: raise young oysters in a cage off a dock or pier, protected from predators, until they're big enough to survive on a restoration reef or be eaten. It's slow, a little messy, and — because a single adult oyster filters roughly 50 gallons of water a day — genuinely useful to the river.

This project is a volunteer effort driven by training and guidance from the Tidewater Oyster Gardeners Association (TOGA), a nonprofit that supplies training and helps individuals find spats, floats, and other stuff needed to raise oysters.

Every cohort gets its own page here, with regular measurements and photos, so you can watch the same 500 oysters grow from spat the size of a fingernail to something worth eating — or, more importantly, worth protecting.

    Filter the water.

    Build reef habitat.

    Oyster shell and living oysters create structure for fish, crabs, and grass shrimp.

  • 3
    Restore what's been lost.

    The Potomac's native reefs are a fraction of their historic size — gardened oysters help rebuild them.
