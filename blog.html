Teaching an AI to Count Oysters

On the same hot week in early August, we tried something new on both cohorts: photographing a full tray of oysters and running an AI count alongside our usual by-hand count, just to see how it would do. The two cohorts gave us opposite answers.

On Cohort 2025, the AI count landed exactly on 103 — matching the manual count oyster for oyster. It was a small, easy tray to photograph: 103 oysters, well spaced, good light. For a first attempt, that was a genuinely encouraging result.

Cohort 2026 told a different story. The manual count came in at 375, but the AI count read only 331 — a gap of 44 oysters, all missing from the same spot. Looking back at the photo, the reason was obvious once we knew to look for it: several oysters in the largest size batch were stacked directly on top of each other, and the AI had counted each overlapping pair as one.

It's a useful early lesson either way. A clean, well-spread tray is something an AI count can apparently handle reliably; a tray with oysters doubled up is not, at least not yet. For now, we're treating the AI count as a quick cross-check rather than a replacement for the manual count — and spreading trays out more deliberately before the next photo.

Full numbers from both checks are on the Cohort 2025 and Cohort 2026 pages.

A Float Breaks Open

At our July 25th check on Cohort 2026, the count came back at 378 — down from 458 the month before. Routine mortality only accounted for 3 of those. The other roughly 78 oysters were gone because one of the cage floats had broken open.

We don't know exactly when the float failed between visits, only that by the time we opened the cage, a whole section had come loose and a large batch of oysters had simply spilled out into the river. It's the single biggest loss this cohort has seen — more than ten times any other check's mortality.

The upside of a spill like this, versus a die-off, is that the oysters aren't necessarily dead — just scattered on the river bottom nearby, where a Chesapeake oyster can actually survive perfectly well on its own. And that's roughly what happened: a month later, on August 26th, we found seven of them on the bottom near the cage and brought them back, more than offsetting that visit's ordinary mortality.

We've since gone over every float and seam on the cage. It's a reminder that a lot of what threatens a garden cage isn't predators or disease — it's just gear wearing out, and needing eyes on it as often as the oysters themselves.

Details from that check, and the recovery, are on the Cohort 2026 page.

A Hard Winter: 405 Down to 142

Cohort 2025 went into last fall at 405 oysters. When we opened the cage for the first full recount of the new season, on May 16th, only 142 remained. That's a loss of nearly two-thirds of the cohort over a single winter — by far the roughest stretch this garden has been through.

We don't have a clean answer for exactly what happened, or when. Between late October and mid-May we only logged mortality checks, not full recounts, so the loss could have been gradual over the whole winter or concentrated in a single bad stretch — a hard freeze, a predator that found its way in, gear that failed quietly under ice. Without a count in between, there's no way to point to one cause with confidence, and we're not going to guess just to have a tidier story.

What we can say is what happened next: the 142 survivors have done well. By early August they'd grown enough for a largest shell of nearly 3 inches, and by September the largest oyster in the cohort measured 3⅞ inches — genuinely close to harvest size. The count has also held essentially flat since May, with zero recorded mortality at three checks in a row.

It's not the outcome we'd have chosen, and it's worth being honest about: a two-thirds loss is a real setback, not a footnote. But it's also a useful data point for next winter — we're planning to log at least one mid-winter check for future cohorts, so a loss like this doesn't go unnoticed until spring.

The full update history for this cohort, including the recovery since, is on the Cohort 2025 page.

Why We Garden Oysters on the Potomac

This site exists to track something pretty small in scope — a few cages of oysters off one dock — that's part of something much bigger: the slow, ongoing effort to bring native oyster reefs back to the Chesapeake Bay watershed.

Oyster reefs in the Bay are a fraction of their historic extent, reduced over more than a century by overharvesting, disease, and habitat loss. Rebuilding them matters for reasons beyond the oysters themselves. A single adult oyster filters somewhere around 50 gallons of water a day, and healthy reefs create structure that shelters fish, crabs, and countless smaller species that have nowhere else to go once the reef is gone.

Oyster gardening is one small, very hands-on piece of that restoration effort. Through the Tidewater Oyster Gardeners Association (TOGA), volunteers like us take in young spat each spring, raise them in protected cages off docks and piers, and hand them back each fall to be planted on sanctuary reefs, where harvesting isn't allowed and the goal is simply for the reef to grow. Raising oysters this way, in a protected cage instead of directly on a reef, dramatically improves their odds of surviving to adulthood, since the biggest threats to young oysters are the predators and competitors that a garden cage keeps mostly at bay — gear failures and hard winters notwithstanding, as it turns out.

We started this particular garden in 2025, with a single small bag of spat from Darryl and more questions than answers. This site is where we're keeping the record — the measurements, the photos, and the occasional surprise — partly so we remember what happened, and partly because we think it's worth sharing what a small, ordinary restoration project actually looks like, month to month, successes and setbacks both.

If you'd like to support this kind of work directly, TOGA accepts donations that fund spat, cages, and reef plantings across the watershed — details on the Donate page.
